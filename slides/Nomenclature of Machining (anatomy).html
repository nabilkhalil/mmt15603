03Cutting edges
2.1 · The two cutting edges

The major edge removes the bulk of the metal. The minor edge decides the finish.

Major cutting edge S

Faces the feed direction. Carries the full uncut chip width b = ap/sin κr, so it takes most of the cutting force and heat.

Minor cutting edge S′

Trails behind, next to the newly machined surface. Together with the nose radius it leaves the feed marks that set surface roughness.

Uncut chip thickness h = f·sin κr. Lowering κr spreads the same chip over a longer edge: thinner chip, lower edge load, but a higher radial force.

06Cut geometry
2.4 · Orthogonal and oblique cutting

Tilt the cutting edge and the chip changes direction.

Orthogonal · λs = 0°

Edge is perpendicular to the cutting velocity. The chip flows straight up the rake face, and only two force components act. This is the 2-D model used for Merchant analysis.

Oblique · λs ≠ 0°

The edge is inclined, so the chip leaves sideways and a third (radial) force appears. Nearly every real turning, milling and drilling operation is oblique.

Research note

Stabler's rule takes the chip-flow angle ηc as roughly equal to λs; later measurements give values up to about 20% smaller because of friction on the rake face. (Meier et al., 2019)

On C45 steel, raising λs from 50° to 85° cut the minimum uncut chip thickness from 12 µm to 4 µm. (Mikołajczyk et al., 2022)

07Rake angle
2.5 · Why the rake angle matters

Raise the rake angle and the shear plane swings up: thinner chip, less force.

Shear angle φ45° + γ/2 − β/2
Chip compression tc/tcos(φ−γ)/sin φ
Shear strain εcot φ + tan(φ−γ)
Cutting force Fcrelative to γ = 0°

Ideal Merchant model: sharp tool, continuous chip, constant shear stress. Real values drift with speed, edge radius and work hardening, but the trends hold and match FEM studies on slide 09.

08Rake angle
2.6 · Classifying the rake angle

Positive, neutral, negative: each trades sharpness for strength.

POSITIVE · γ > 0°
Function
Sharp wedge shears the metal easily; chip slides away freely.
Effect
Lower force and power, lower temperature, thinner chip.
Risk
Weaker edge loaded in bending; chipping on hard or interrupted cuts.
Use
Aluminium, copper, soft steels, finishing, low-rigidity setups.
NEUTRAL · γ = 0°
Function
Rake face perpendicular to cutting velocity; simple to grind.
Effect
Moderate force; the reference case in many models.
Risk
Neither the free cutting of positive nor the strength of negative.
Use
Brass, form tools, threading tools, teaching and FEM baselines.
NEGATIVE · γ < 0°
Function
Thick wedge puts the edge in compression, which carbide and CBN tolerate well.
Effect
Higher force and heat; tends to leave compressive residual stress.
Risk
Needs a rigid machine and more power; vibration on slender parts.
Use
Hardened steel (>45 HRC), cast iron, interrupted cuts, double-sided inserts.
09Evidence
2.7 · What the research shows

Simulation and experiment agree on the rake-angle trends.

FEM · SiCp/Al composite

As the rake angle increased, cutting temperature and cutting force fell while the shear angle rose clearly.

Zhou & Huang (2010)
FEM · DEFORM-2D

A larger rake angle produced a thinner chip with tighter curl, consistent with the higher shear angle.

Fang & Zeng (2004)
FEM · interaction study

Rake angle, clearance angle and edge radius interact; choosing one in isolation can mislead the force and temperature prediction.

Zhang et al. (2007)
Experiment · rake & approach angle

Main cutting force and tool-tip temperature were measured together across rake and approach angles, showing both angles must be chosen as a pair.

Sağlam et al. (2006)
Hard turning · CBN

CBN tools for hardened steel are made with negative rake geometry so the brittle edge works in compression.

Adamik et al. (2013)
Surface integrity · bearings

Hard turning with negative-rake inserts is associated with compressive residual stress, which helps fatigue life.

Sağır et al. (2022, preprint)

Discussion: these studies use different materials and methods. Which conclusions would you trust for AISI 1045 steel, and what extra test would you run to confirm them?

10Clearance
2.8 · Clearance angle and the wedge budget

Rake, clearance and wedge always share the same 90°.

  • Clearance α stops the flank rubbing on the freshly cut surface. Too small: friction, heat and fast flank wear. Too large: a thin, weak edge.
  • Typical values: about 5–8° for steels with carbide; larger for soft, ductile metals.
  • Flank wear growth raised tool and workpiece temperatures sharply and increased thrust force much more than cutting force; heating accelerated past VB ≈ 0.3 mm. (Zhao et al., 2007)
  • A larger cutting-edge radius increased the wear rate, most strongly during initial cut-in. (Kountanya & Endres, 2004)

Wedge budget: γ + α + β = 90°

2.9 · Nose radius, feed and surface finish

Feed marks are copies of the nose. Halve the feed and Ra drops by four.

Typical classISO 1302 roughness grade

Ra at this feed, by nose radius

  • Regression on maraging-steel turning found feed and nose radius to be the significant factors for roughness. (Ying et al., 2007)
  • The formula is a floor. Built-up edge, vibration and wear push measured Ra above it.
  • A large rε is not free: when ap is smaller than rε, the radial force becomes dominant and can trigger chatter. (Bartarya & Choudhury, 2011)
12Machinability
2.10 · Machinability

Machinability is a system property, not a single number.

The ease with which a material can be cut to the required quality at acceptable cost, judged by tool life, forces and power, chip form, cutting temperature, surface finish and integrity, and burr formation. (Festas et al., 2021)

Material propertyTypical effect when it rises
Hardness, strengthHigher force and power, faster abrasive wear
Thermal conductivityHeat leaves the cut zone; tool runs cooler. Al 6061-T6 ≈ 173 W/m·K vs Ti-6Al-4V ≈ 7 W/m·K
DuctilityLong continuous chips, built-up edge, poor finish
Work hardeningHard surface layer, notch wear at depth-of-cut line
Chemical reactivityAdhesion and diffusion wear (titanium)
Free-cutting additions (S, Pb, Bi)Chip breaking, lower friction, better rating
How ratings are made

A machinability index compares materials under one fixed test. Standard production-scale tests such as ASTM E618 were extended so brasses, carbon and leaded steels and aluminium alloys could be ranked on one scale. (Kundig et al., 1990)

Indices are tied to their test: Davim & Mata (2005) built a separate index for turning fibre-reinforced plastics, because metal criteria did not transfer.

Difficult-to-cut case

Titanium alloys rank low because of low thermal conductivity, high chemical reactivity and high strength at cutting temperature, which together drive rapid tool wear. (Festas et al., 2021)
