04Design of experiments
4.2 · How the studies find "the most suitable parameters"

An orthogonal array balances every level, so averaging three runs isolates one factor.

Click a level to highlight its three runs

Three quantities you will compute

Main effect of level j = mean of the 3 runs at that level. The plot of these means is the "main effects plot".

Sum of squares SSfactor = 3 · Σ (level mean − grand mean)²

Contribution = SSfactor / SStotal × 100%. With 9 runs and 3 factors, what is left over is error and interactions.

Full factorial: 3³ = 27 runs. L9: 9 runs. The price is that interactions cannot be separated from error, so check the conclusions with a confirmation run.

  • Akkuş & Yaka linked higher feed to thicker undeformed chips, more tool vibration and worse finish, and found energy use rose at high feed.

08Cutting force
4.6 · Effect of cutting parameters on cutting force

Depth raises force in proportion. Feed raises it less than proportionally. Speed and rake lower it a little.

Kienzle force model

Fc = kc · ap · f, kc = kc1.1 · h−mc
Cutting force FcN
Specific force kcN/mm²
Double ap×2.00Fc change
Double fFc change
  • Speed: forces usually fall a little as speed rises, often explained by thermal softening in the shear zones. Strain rate rises at the same time, so this is a simplification.
  • Rake angle: a larger rake lowers both tangential and normal forces, the normal force proportionally more.
  • Approach angle, clearance angle, corner radius: virtually no effect on force magnitude in the practical range.

Model constants for a low-carbon steel: kc1.1 = 1700 N/mm², mc = 0.25, κr = 90°, about 1% change in kc per degree of rake, and a small illustrative speed factor. Use supplier data for real jobs.

09Temperature
4.7 · Effect of cutting parameters on cutting temperature

Speed is usually the strongest driver of temperature, but the studies do not agree on the order.

Rank the parameters, then reveal what each study found

Pick a rank for speed, feed and depth in your row first.

  • Most cutting power turns into heat. Only about 10–20% of it goes into the tool; the chip carries most of the rest. (Abou-El-Hossein & Kops, 2017)
  • Disagreement comes from different materials, tools, measurement methods (tool–work thermocouple, embedded thermocouple, IR camera) and ranges tested.

Where the heat goes as speed rises

Schematic shares, not measured data. At higher speed there is less time for heat to conduct into the work, so a larger share leaves with the chip, while the absolute temperature at the tool–chip interface still rises.

12Chip geometry
4.10 · Measuring a saw-tooth chip

Nickel alloys form serrated chips. Three measurements describe them.

Equivalent thicknessmm · h + (H − h)/2
Segmentation Gs(H − h) / H
Chip reduction ζheq / t
  • Saw-tooth distance Pc is the distance between two adjacent teeth.
  • A lower chip reduction coefficient means less chip deformation, so less energy per unit volume; the PVD tool cut ζ by 22% in Case study 1.
  • In FEM and experiments on GH4169 (Inconel 718 equivalent), both Gs and Pc increased with cutting speed. (Zhang et al., 2007)

PVD (TiAlN + TiN) inserts gave lower cutting force and wear than CVD (TiCN + Al₂O₃ + TiN), attributed to the thin coating. But the PVD insert failed catastrophically after 18 min of dry cutting at 175 m/min, while the CVD insert, with its slightly rounded edge, kept cutting. MQL improved both.

14Improving machinability
4.12 · Route (a) · free-machining additives

Additives create weak points in the shear zone, so the chip breaks with less energy.

  • Chip breakage: shorter chips clear the cutting zone, avoid re-cutting and reduce downtime for chip cleaning.
  • Interfaces: at high speed, better friction and anti-adhesion conditions raise tool life and lower temperature and residual stress.
  • Low-speed cutting of difficult materials: less built-up edge, so lower roughness and adhesion wear.
15Improving machinability
4.13 · Route (b) · microstructure modification by heat treatment

Match the microstructure to the job: soft enough to cut, not so soft that it smears.

  • Annealing AISI 4340 lowered its microhardness and produced coarse pearlite; the authors call annealing a low-cost way to improve machinability of alloy steel. (Wagri et al., 2023)
  • Selenium additions helped quenched-and-tempered 4140 but not the as-hot-rolled steel: additive and microstructure work together. (Zhang et al., 2020)
