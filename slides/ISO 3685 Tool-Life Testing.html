03The test
5.1 · What ISO 3685 standardises

Seven steps from a fresh insert to a tool-life equation. Click a step.

Scope

Tool-life testing of single-point turning tools of high-speed steel, sintered carbide and ceramic, cutting steel and cast iron. It fixes work material, tool, conditions, wear measurement and evaluation so results are comparable.

ISO 3685:1993 is the current edition. The steps here summarise the procedure; use the standard itself for the exact clauses.

08Criteria
5.6 · Tool-life criteria

Tool life ends at whichever limit is reached first.

Race to the limit · carbide insert

illustrative wear curves

Criteria by tool material

Sintered carbide: VBB = 0.3 mm if wear is regular in zone B; VBB max = 0.6 mm if irregular; crater depth KT = 0.06 + 0.3f mm.

HSS and ceramics: catastrophic failure, or the same VBB / VBB max limits.

10Wear curve
5.8 · Recording the wear curve

Stop and measure often enough to catch the end of the steady-wear stage.

True Tmin at VBB 0.3
Interpolated Tfrom your points
Error%
  • T is read by linear interpolation between the last point below the criterion and the first point above it.
  • Each stop costs time and can disturb the cut, so shorten the interval as the curve steepens, not from the start.
  • Record the wear form at every stop, not only the number. A jump in VB can mean chipping, not wear.

12Reliability
5.10 · Tool life is a distribution, not a number

Identical inserts, identical conditions, different lives. Plan tool changes on the early failures.

  • Serra & Rmili ran ISO 3685 tests on thirty inserts from one production batch under strictly identical conditions to study wear development statistically. (Serra & Rmili, 2016)
  • The Weibull distribution is widely used for cutting-tool life and reliability. (Yang et al., 2020, preprint)
  • A low shape β means wide scatter: changing tools at the mean life would let many fail in the cut.

Simulated samples. β values for real tools depend on tool, work material and wear mode.

13Critical view
5.11 · Where the standard is debated

ISO 3685 makes results comparable. It does not make them universal.

Same T–vc treatment for all tool materials

Panda and Duplák argue the standard evaluates all tool materials with the same Taylor-based characteristics. Their ceramic-tool tests on C60 steel did not follow the standard's dependence.

Panda et al. (2013); Duplák et al. (2015)
Flank-wear criterion vs part quality

Basing tool life on the surface roughness the part needs, instead of a fixed VB, gave on average 23% longer tool life; VB at end of life ranged from 0.36 to 0.54 mm.

Iqbal et al. (2022)
Other operations, other limits

A milling study on Ti6Al4V set its wear limit at 0.2 mm and found each 1% rise in cutting speed cut tool life by about 4%. Criteria are chosen per process.

Tatar & Sjöberg (2020)
Measuring needs the machine to stop

Direct optical measurement requires stopping and is time-consuming, which motivates monitoring flank wear through cutting-force signals.

Equeter et al. (2018)

Discussion: for a batch of aerospace parts with Ra ≤ 0.8 µm, would you end tool life at VBB = 0.3 mm or at a roughness limit? What evidence would you collect?
