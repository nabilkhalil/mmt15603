3.3 · Cutting speed and spindle speed

Spindle speed is set on the machine. Cutting speed is what the tool edge feels.

V in m/min, D in mm, N in rev/min. The 1000 converts millimetres to metres.

06Machine
3.4 · Machine variables: power, torque, rigidity

The machine sets the ceiling. If it cannot supply the power, speed, feed and depth must come down.

Will this cut fit the lathe?

Pc = vc·ap·f·kc / 60 000
Cutting powerkW at the tool
Motor power neededkW (η = 0.8)
Cutting force FckN
Removal rate Qcm³/min
  • Power and torque limit the removal rate. At low spindle speed the torque limit usually bites first.
  • Rigidity of machine, holder, tool and part controls deflection and vibration. Lack of dynamic stiffness leads to regenerative chatter: rough surface, noise and accelerated tool wear. (Coba Salcedo et al., 2018)
  • Stability lobe diagrams mark the speed and depth combinations that cut without chatter, so removal rate can be raised safely. (Coba Salcedo et al., 2018)
  • Accuracy of slides and spindle sets the best tolerance the process can hold, whatever the tool.

kc values here are indicative mid-range figures for h ≈ 0.2–0.4 mm. For real jobs use the insert supplier's kc data, which corrects for chip thickness and rake angle.

08Cutting speed
3.6 · Cutting conditions: speed and tool life

Cutting speed has the greatest influence on tool life: V·Tⁿ = C.

Speed for T = 15 minm/min

  • Small n means a steep penalty: with n = 0.125, a 20% speed rise cuts life by more than 75%.
  • Turning AISI 4140 with coated and ceramic inserts, tool life was more sensitive to cutting speed than to feed. (Paengchit & Saikaew, 2023)
  • n and C are not constants of nature. They must be fitted for each tool, work material and wear criterion.

Preset n and C are typical textbook values for turning plain carbon steel, for comparison only.

09Surface finish
3.7 · Cutting speed, built-up edge and finish

Raising the speed usually improves finish because the built-up edge disappears.

Built-up edge cycle

schematic, not measured data

Experiment · mild steel, dry turning

In a 384-run full factorial study, the best roughness came from low feed, large nose radius and high cutting speed. Built-up edge worsened roughness at specific speed, feed and nose-radius levels, and depth of cut stopped mattering above 160 m/min.

Masounave et al. (1997)
Experiment · low-carbon steels

The built-up edge disappeared as speed rose to 65–80 m/min, and roughness improved. Carbon content also changed how strongly the edge formed.

Shather (2009)

Three-way interactions mattered in Masounave et al.: reading main effects alone could lead to the wrong conclusion. Design experiments accordingly.

11Work material
3.9 · Work material variables

Same machine, same tool: the work material can still change tool life several times over.

Hardness & tensile strength

Higher strength raises cutting force, power and abrasive wear. Very soft, ductile metals swing the other way: long chips and built-up edge.

Chemical composition

In free-cutting steels, lead gathers at manganese sulphides and acts as an internal lubricant, lowering friction and heat. (Byrne et al., 2003)

Inclusions & cleanliness

Among carburizing steels, the grade with the most sulphur gave the longest tool life; MnS inclusions are thought to act as stress raisers in the shear zone. (Ånmark et al., 2015)

Carbon content

In low-carbon steels, higher carbon reduced built-up edge and improved roughness at moderate speed. (Shather, 2009)

Microstructure

Hard phases (carbides, martensite) abrade the tool; ferrite is soft but sticky; lamellar pearlite and spheroidised structures cut differently at the same hardness.

Production route & cold work

Cold drawing raises hardness and lowers ductility, often giving shorter, better-broken chips. Castings bring sand inclusions and hard skin; additively built parts bring anisotropy.

Discussion: the "clean steel" in Ånmark et al. had fewer inclusions, which helps fatigue strength but hurt tool life. How would you balance part performance against machinability?

12Chip shape
3.10 · Assessment criterion: chip shape

Is each chip form acceptable on an unattended CNC lathe?

  • Chip shape decides whether chips clear the tool or wrap around the part, holder and operator.
  • Chip breakers work by adding plastic deformation in the secondary zone, work-hardening the chip so it becomes brittle and snaps. (Zhang et al., 2013)
  • Spray cooling gave better chip breakability, smoother finish and longer tool life than dry cutting of 416 stainless steel. (Byrne et al., 2003)

Chip-form names follow the ISO 3685 tool-life testing standard's chip classification.

13Tool life
3.11 · Assessment criterion: tool life

Tool life is not a property of the tool. It is the time to reach the wear limit you choose.

Flank wear VB against cutting time

illustrative curve
Tool life Tmin to reach VB limit
Zone at the limitwear stage
  • ISO 3685 sets common criteria for carbide: average flank wear VB = 0.3 mm, or maximum VB = 0.6 mm when wear is irregular.
  • Judging tool life by the surface roughness actually needed, rather than a fixed VB, gave on average 23% longer tool life in face turning of alloy steel. (Iqbal et al., 2022)
  • Vibration signals tracked flank-wear growth closely in ISO 3685 tool-life tests, which opens the way to in-process monitoring. (Serra & Rmili, 2016)
14Index
3.12 · From tool-life tests to a machinability rating

A rating compares the speed each material allows for the same tool life.

Reference V60m/min
Test V60m/min
Index% of reference
I = V60(test) / V60(ref) × 100

V60 = C / 60ⁿ is the speed giving 60 minutes of tool life. The reference here is an example line (n = 0.25, C = 400); classic AISI ratings use free-machining steel AISI 1212 as 100%.

  • Ratings change when tool grades improve, so machinability data must be updated regularly.
  • Current data come from machinability-testing laboratories and from tool makers' insert grade advisers.
